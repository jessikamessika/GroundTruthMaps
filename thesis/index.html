Gully Erosion on Lakes Huron and Erie

How fast are coastal gullies growing, and how much sediment do they put into the lake? I answered this by combining 90 years of aerial imagery with UAV-built elevation models, and by putting error bars on every number.

Workflow

Two data streams, one set of rates. Historical imagery gives area over time. Fresh drone surveys give volume. Both feed a sediment budget.

Method, step by step

  1. Digitize history. I traced gully outlines by hand in ArcGIS Pro for 1955, 1966, 1978, 1980, 2006, 2010, 2015 and 2020, after georeferencing every image to the 2020 SWOOP imagery.
  2. Fit growth rates. A linear fit of area against year (SLOPE in Excel) gave annual areal growth. Projecting the fit back to zero area (INTERCEPT) estimated each gully's initiation year.
  3. Survey by drone. An Autel EVO II V3 RTK flew programmed plan-view surveys at 70 to 80% overlap, 60 m above the bluff, with oblique photos added by hand. White 30 cm GCPs, surveyed with an Emlid Reach RS2, were laid every 10 to 100 m.
  4. Build elevation models. Pix4D Structure from Motion produced DEMs. Gully volumes came from the ArcGIS Surface Volume tool.
  5. Convert to rates. Current volume divided by years since initiation gave average annual sediment discharge. I also expressed results as parking spots and garbage trucks per year so planners could picture them.

Large gullies grow faster in absolute terms. Relative to their size, small gullies grow faster. Growth is highest early and tapers, so new large gullies deserve the closest monitoring.

How I handled uncertainty

Every imagery year has its own positional error, so I combined two sources, following Hapke and Reid (2007). Georeferencing error came from the RMSE against the 2020 reference. Digitization error came from each dataset's resolution and an estimated 1 to 3 pixel user error, larger for finer imagery.

To turn that into an error on area, I buffered each gully outline outward and inward by the combined uncertainty. The difference between the two polygons is the areal error for that gully and year.

Limits worth knowing: two remote sites had GPS dropouts and dense canopy, so a DEM there would have mapped vegetation, not ground, and those gullies were studied from imagery only. One gully has since been remediated.
